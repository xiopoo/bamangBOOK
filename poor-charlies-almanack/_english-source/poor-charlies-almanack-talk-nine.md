# Poor Charlie's Almanack — Talk Nine: Academic Economics: Strengths and Faults After Considering Interdisciplinary Needs

**来源：** https://www.stripe.press/poor-charlies-almanack/talk-nine

## 编者导言

October 3, 2003 — Herb Kay Undergraduate Lecture, University of California, Santa Barbara Economics Department

What Charlie laid forth on that occasion might be considered the Grand Unified Theory of the Munger approach. The talk incorporates the many ideas that Charlie discussed in his previous talks and presents them in the context of one academic discipline, economics.

Charlie's audience, the economics department of this major university, was the perfect group on which to unleash this lament—and remediation proposals besides—about the lack of multidisciplinarianism in academia. Economics, after all, is the queen of the soft sciences.

## 演讲正文

I have outlined some remarks in a rough way, and after I'm finished talking from that outline, I'll take questions as long as anybody can endure listening.

As you might guess, I agreed to do this because the subject of getting the soft sciences so they talk better to each other has been one that has interested me for decades. And, of course, economics is, in many respects, the queen of the soft sciences. It's the most fundamental and well-developed of subjects that are inherently adumbrated.

One interesting fact you are entitled to know is that I never took a course in economics.

### Berkshire and Efficient-Market Theory

For a long time, there was a Nobel Prize–winning economist who explained Berkshire Hathaway's success as follows: Berkshire beat the market through one sigma of luck because nobody could beat the market except by luck. This hard-form version of efficient-market theory was then believed by most economists.

Then, the economist reversed the explanation 180 degrees. He said, "No, it was still six sigmas, but it was six sigmas of skill." This history demonstrates the truth of Benjamin Franklin's observation: "If you would persuade, appeal to interest and not to reason." The man changed his view when his own interest made the change imperative.

Berkshire's whole record has been achieved without paying one ounce of attention to the efficient-market theory in its hard form.

### The Failings of Economics

Charlie goes on at length about:
- **Physics envy** in economics—the craving to reduce complex systems to Newtonian formulas
- The limits of **Gaussian distributions** in modeling financial markets
- The need for economics to incorporate **psychology** (especially the work of Cialdini, Kahneman, and Tversky), **biology** (Darwinian natural selection), and other hard sciences
- The folly of **"one-legged" thinking** and the importance of a **multidisciplinary latticework of mental models**
- Criticism of **modern portfolio theory**, the **Capital Asset Pricing Model**, and the overreliance on mathematical elegance over real-world validity
- The importance of **checklists** in complex decision-making
- The virtues of **"take what you wish"** across disciplines while maintaining proper attribution and fundamentalness order

### Specific Examples from Charlie's Career

Charlie discusses specific examples where multidisciplinary thinking gave him an edge:
- The **Coca-Cola** case study
- The **See's Candies** acquisition
- Various insurance and credit decisions at Berkshire
- The importance of understanding **incentive-caused bias**, **social proof**, and the **consistency principle** in evaluating businesses and management

### Q&A Session

The talk included an extensive Q&A covering:
- Trade policy and comparative advantage
- Macroeconomics and forecasting
- Behavioral economics
- Economic modeling limitations
- The future of economics as a discipline

## Talk Nine — Revisited

This waggish talk on economics, given in 2003, gave me pleasure when I composed it and when I delivered it. And I still like both the talk's general approach and most of its content.

Over the years since this talk, the efficient-market theory has fallen from grace, as I predicted. Few now believe the hard form of the theory that Berkshire Hathaway's success was just luck. Behavioral economics, led by Daniel Kahneman and the late Amos Tversky, has gained tremendous ground, incorporating psychology into economic models. And the 2008 financial crisis showed, in dramatic fashion, the flaws in the Gaussian-based models that dominated Wall Street's risk management.

But while I applaud the progress, I remain unsatisfied. Behavioral economics, as practiced in academia, is still too narrow. It has adopted only a few psychological insights—primarily those that can be demonstrated in clever experiments—while ignoring the broader psychology of human misjudgment that I have spent a lifetime cataloging. The checklist approach I recommend, with its two dozen or so standard causes of misjudgment, remains far more comprehensive than what is taught in behavioral economics courses.

Nevertheless, I am grateful for what progress has been made. The world is a better place when economists know a little psychology, and when investors know that markets are not perfectly efficient. As usual, my advice remains: master the best that other people have figured out, across multiple disciplines, and use checklists to avoid the most common errors. It's not that hard, it's a lot of fun, and the rewards—financial and otherwise—are immense.
