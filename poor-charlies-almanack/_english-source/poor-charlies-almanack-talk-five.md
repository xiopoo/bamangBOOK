## 编者导言

Having ranted in the previous speech about all that is wrong in academia, Charlie holds forth here on the solutions. Delivered in 1998 at the 50th reunion of his Harvard Law School class, this talk focuses on a hugely complicated issue—the narrowness of elite education—and segments it into elements whose solutions, when taken together, form a satisfactory answer to the problem.

Through a series of rhetorical questions, Charlie posits that professionals such as attorneys, to their own detriment, lack multidisciplinary skills. From his own extensive multidisciplinary studies, he recognizes that there are "subconscious mental tendencies" that keep people from broadening their own horizons sufficiently. Nonetheless, he brings unique and memorable solutions to the problem.

This talk—a favorite of your editor—clearly demonstrates Charlie's "uncommon common sense." He says, "When it really matters, as with pilots and surgeons, educational systems employ highly effective structures. Yet they don't employ these same well-understood structures in other areas of learning that are also important."

## 演讲正文（摘要）

Charlie opens by engaging in "Socratic solitaire," asking and answering five questions:

### 1. Do broadscale professionals need more multidisciplinary skill?

Charlie identifies two key psychological tendencies that impair professional judgment: incentive-caused bias and man-with-a-hammer tendency. The partial cure is obvious—a broad set of skills across multiple disciplines limits bad cognitive effects.

"If A is narrow professional doctrine and B consists of the big, extra-useful concepts from other disciplines, then clearly the professional possessing A plus B will usually be better off than the poor possessor of A alone."

### 2. Was our education too unidisciplinary?

Yes. Broadscale problems cross many academic disciplines, yet education remains balkanized. Alfred North Whitehead warned of "the fatal unconnectedness of academic disciplines." Modern academia has made some progress, with figures like E. O. Wilson (Harvard) and Linus Pauling (Caltech) fighting unconnectedness at disciplinary borders.

### 3. What is the goal of best-form multidisciplinarity?

Charlie uses **pilot training** as the model. Pilots are trained in a strict six-element system to avoid man-with-a-hammer tendency. The same system, scaled up with expanded coverage, should apply to broadscale education:
- Multidisciplinary coverage of immense amplitude
- All needed skills raised to practice-based fluency
- Considerable power of synthesis at disciplinary boundaries
- Forward and reverse thinking techniques
- Checklist routines as a permanent part of knowledge systems

This task, though daunting, is not impossible because:
1. Only the truly big ideas in each discipline carry most of the freight
2. Elite education has ample talent and time (top 1% students, 13 years)
3. Inversion and checklists are easily learned

### 4. How much has elite soft-science education been corrected?

Some progress has been made—law schools brought in negotiation (Roger Fisher), economics, and game theory. Economics adopted the "tragedy of the commons" from biology. But the "take what you wish" approach has defects, including un-attributed borrowings and failure to recognize fundamentalness order.

### 5. What practices would hasten progress?

1. **More mandatory courses** (not optional) — psychology and accounting should be required in legal education
2. **Multi-discipline problem-solving practice** — like aircraft simulators, testing across disciplines. Charlie cites a Harvard Business School professor who gave a test about two old ladies inheriting a shoe factory—the winning answer used agency costs and marginal utility from undergraduate economics and psychology
3. **Use of best business periodicals** (Wall Street Journal, Forbes, Fortune) as simulators for multidisciplinary thinking
4. **Avoid professors and students of strong, passionate political ideology** — objectivity is essential
5. **Imitate the fundamental organizing ethos of hard science** (math, physics, chemistry, engineering):
   - Full attribution for cross-disciplinary takings
   - Mandatory preference for the most fundamental explanation

Charlie's own life serves as evidence: "I have never taken any course, anywhere, in chemistry, economics, psychology, or business. But I early took elementary physics and math and paid enough attention to somehow assimilate the fundamental organizing ethos of hard science."

He concludes with Dr. Samuel Johnson's word for maintenance of removable ignorance: **"treachery."** And he promises that the happier mental realm of multidisciplinarity "is one from which no one willingly returns. A return would be like cutting off one's hands."

## Talk Five — Revisited

As I review Talk Five in 2006, I would not change a word. And I continue to believe that my ideas are important. In my attitude I may be displaying too much similarity to my long-dead relative, Reverend Theodore Munger, former chaplain of Yale. Theodore published a collection of his sermons, laying out proper conduct with a strong, ex cathedra tone. Then, late in life, he published a final edition, reporting in his foreword that he had made no changes at all and was now producing the new edition only because the extreme popularity of his sermons had caused excessive wear in the original printing plates.
